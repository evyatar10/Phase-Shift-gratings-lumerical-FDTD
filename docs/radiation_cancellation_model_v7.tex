\section{Summary}
\textbf{The question.} Johnson et al.\ (2001) raised the $Q$ of a photonic crystal cavity by cancelling the one
spherical harmonic (multipole) that carried most of its radiation. Can we do the same for our cavity, for example
with a second set of teeth, at a fixed 20\,$\mu$m mode width?

\textbf{The answer.}
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
\item \textbf{Not one harmonic at a time.} Our radiation is spread over five harmonic groups; the largest holds 19\,\%
(M). Cancelling it perfectly would raise the radiation $Q$ by at most $1/(1-0.19)=1.24\times$. Worse, the table is not
even unique: moving the centre of the expansion by 0.19\,$\mu$m changes the dipole share from 3 to 19\,\%.
\item \textbf{But the leak of the plain TE device is compact.} 83\,\% of its radiation pattern can be reproduced by
sources within $\pm0.5$\,$\mu$m of the $\pi$ shift, i.e.\ the innermost tooth on each side (D). So the right target is
not a harmonic but \emph{the junction}: redesign the few teeth at the $\pi$ shift so that they radiate the leak's
pattern with the opposite sign. This 83\,\% is a ceiling; an independent estimate of what real teeth can do is about
10\,\%. One short run decides between them.
\item \textbf{The apodized (overshoot) device} radiates from many places along the grating; that is a job for the
inverse design. \textbf{Plain TM} sends two thirds of its leak along the waveguide axis; that is a job for a periodic
comb, as already found experimentally (+16\,\% $Q$).
\item \textbf{A second set of teeth} (a second harmonic of the corrugation) radiates outside the light cone and is not
a direct lever.
\item \textbf{Envelope shaping remains the big lever}: the apodized device radiates at least $15\times$ less than the
plain one at the same mode width.
\item \textbf{The far field measurement had an error}, now found and fixed: the two flat monitors were each correct only
within $40^\circ$ of their normal. For TE the corrected results are almost identical; for TM the details changed.
\item \textbf{For the Q3dB devices} the harmonic percentages are expected to stay the same when the device is lengthened,
and a cancellation of a fraction $f$ of the leak raises $Q_{3\rm dB}$ by $1/(1-f)$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The device and the setting}
A SiN waveguide core ($n=1.97$, 350\,nm high, 800\,nm average width) in oxide ($n_c=1.444$), with sidewall corrugation
and a $\pi$ phase shift at $x=0$; $x$ runs along the waveguide, $y$ sideways, $z$ up. At 1559\,nm the wavenumber in
oxide is $k=5.82$\,$\mu$m$^{-1}$ ($1/k=0.17$\,$\mu$m). The guided wave has $\beta=6.28$\,$\mu$m$^{-1}$ (TE, pitch 500\,nm),
just above $k$: on its own it cannot radiate (it is outside the ``light cone''). Radiation comes only from what breaks the
perfect periodic wave: the finite envelope of the mode, its sharp kink at the $\pi$ shift, and the device ends.

Three devices were studied, all with $N=98$ periods per side and a 19 to 20\,$\mu$m mode:
\textbf{A} plain TE (corrugation 250\,nm), \textbf{B} TE with Itai's ``overshoot'' apodization, \textbf{C} plain TM
(corrugation 325\,nm, pitch 516.83\,nm).

\section{The model: where radiation comes from}
\textbf{One exact formula.} Rewrite Maxwell's equations so that everything that differs from uniform oxide is a source:
the core is a sheet of oscillating polarization $\Delta\varepsilon\,\mathbf E$ ($\Delta\varepsilon=\varepsilon-n_c^2=1.80$ in
SiN) radiating into uniform oxide. The far field is then exactly
\begin{equation}
\mathbf A(\hat{\mathbf r})=(\mathsf I-\hat{\mathbf r}\hat{\mathbf r})\int\Delta\varepsilon\,\mathbf E\,e^{-ik\hat{\mathbf r}\cdot\mathbf r}\,d^3r,
\qquad \frac{dP}{d\Omega}\propto|\mathbf A|^2,\qquad Q_{\rm rad}=\frac{\omega U}{P}.
\end{equation}
In words: the radiation in direction $\hat{\mathbf r}$ is the Fourier transform of the source at the spatial frequency
$k\hat{\mathbf r}$. Only Fourier content with $|k_x|<k$ radiates. In our simulations (uniform oxide, no substrate) this is
exact, and it is \emph{linear}: two sources add their fields.

\textbf{Spherical harmonics (multipoles).} Any far field can be written as a sum of electric and magnetic multipoles of
order $l$ (dipole $l=1$, quadrupole $l=2$, \dots) and index $m$. Their powers add:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf A=\sum_{lm}[\alpha_{lm}\mathbf X_{lm}+\beta_{lm}\hat{\mathbf r}\times\mathbf X_{lm}],\qquad P\propto\sum_{lm}(|\alpha_{lm}|^2+|\beta_{lm}|^2),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\alpha_{lm}=4\pi(-i)^l\!\int\Delta\varepsilon\,j_l(kr)\,\mathbf X^*_{lm}\cdot\mathbf E\,d^3r,\qquad
\beta_{lm}=\frac{4\pi i(-i)^l}{k}\!\int\Delta\varepsilon\,\mathbf E\cdot\nabla\times[j_l(kr)\mathbf X^*_{lm}]\,d^3r .
\end{equation}
This is Johnson's argument: if one term is removed, its power is gone, whatever the others do. The key rule is that a
source of size $R$ needs orders up to $l\approx kR$, because $j_l(kr)\sim(kr)^l$. A point source radiates only $l=1$; a
source spread over one tooth ($kR\approx1.5$) already needs $l$ up to 2 or 3. The dipole share can be read without any
expansion: $f_{\rm ED}=\frac{3}{8\pi}|\int\mathbf A\,d\Omega|^2/\int|\mathbf A|^2d\Omega$ (needs the complex vector far field;
an intensity map that ``looks like a dipole'' proves nothing).

\textbf{Symmetry (corrected from v6.2).} The device is mirror symmetric in $y$ and $z$, so the radiation has a definite
parity in those two directions. For TE this allows electric multipoles with $l+m$ even and magnetic ones with $l+m$ odd;
TM the converse. v6.2 also assumed a mirror in $x$; there is none (both arms are built narrow then wide in the same
order, and the standing wave has a node at $x=-0.125$\,$\mu$m and a maximum at $+0.125$\,$\mu$m, M). A feature that breaks
the $y$ or $z$ mirror radiates into channels the mode does not use, so features should be symmetric in $y$ and $z$;
their $x$ position is free.

\section{The model: how an added feature changes the radiation}
\textbf{Three first order terms.} If a feature (hole, post, changed tooth) changes the dielectric by $\delta\varepsilon_P$
($-1.80$ for an oxide hole in SiN, $+1.80$ for a SiN post in oxide),
\begin{equation}
\mathbf A_{\rm new}-\mathbf A_c=\underbrace{\mathcal R[\delta\varepsilon_P\mathbf E_0]}_{\text{the feature radiates}}
+\underbrace{\mathcal R[\Delta\varepsilon\,\delta\mathbf E]}_{\text{the cavity field changes}}
+\underbrace{\delta k\,\partial_k\mathcal R[\Delta\varepsilon\mathbf E_0]}_{\text{the resonance shifts}} .
\end{equation}
The first term has a closed form and is used to screen ideas without simulation. The second and third need a run.
Both earlier successes worked mostly through the second term (Sec.~8).

\textbf{A small feature is a dipole.} A feature smaller than about 0.15\,$\mu$m radiates like a dipole
$\mathbf p=\varepsilon_0\delta\varepsilon_PV_PL\,\mathbf E_0(\mathbf r_p)$ placed at $\mathbf r_p$ ($L=1.30$ is the field
enhancement inside a vertical oxide hole in SiN). At the centre this is a pure $l=1$ pattern. Moved a distance $x_p$ away,
the same dipole spreads its power over many orders, because the phase factor $e^{-ik\hat{\mathbf r}\cdot\mathbf r_p}$
mixes them:
\begin{center}\small\begin{tabular}{cccccccc}\hline
$kx_p$ & $l=1$ & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 & 7\\\hline
0 & 1.00 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
1 & 0.86 & 0.13 & 0.01 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
2 & 0.55 & 0.36 & 0.08 & 0.01 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
4 & 0.14 & 0.29 & 0.33 & 0.18 & 0.05 & 0.01 & 0\\
6 & 0.06 & 0.10 & 0.16 & 0.26 & 0.24 & 0.13 & 0.04\\\hline
\end{tabular}\\[2pt]Table 1: share of a dipole's power in each order $l$ versus its distance $x_p$ from the origin.\end{center}
One tooth from the centre is already $kx_p\approx1.5$. \emph{This table is the reason a real feature cannot be told to
radiate one harmonic.}

\textbf{Pairs, combs and the phase problem.} To cancel radiation, a feature must produce the same pattern with the
opposite \emph{complex} amplitude.
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item A feature inside the core is driven by a standing wave, which is real up to one global phase: it has a sign and
a size, nothing else. A symmetric pair at $\pm x_p$ adds a factor $2\cos(kx_p\sin\theta\cos\varphi)$, again without a
continuous phase.
\item A periodic comb of period $\Lambda_2$ and offset $\delta x$ radiates into a narrow band of angles at
$u_x=(\beta-K_2)/k$, $K_2=2\pi/\Lambda_2$, with a phase $\phi_2=2\pi\delta x/\Lambda_2$ that can be dialled. To aim at a
direction $u_x$: $\Lambda_2=2\pi/(\beta+k|u_x|)$.
\item A feature in the cladding, driven by the leaky travelling field, gets a phase from its position.
\item Moving a tooth boundary is not a small volume change: use the continuous fields $\mathbf E_\parallel$ and
$D_\perp$ (Johnson et al.\ 2002). A tooth shift $\delta x$ gives a response $\propto ik_x$ times the source, with a
direction dependent phase.
\item A second set of teeth at the second harmonic of the pitch, acting on the $\pm\beta$ wave, produces Fourier content
near $\pm3\beta$, outside the light cone. It can only help through what happens near the junction.
\end{itemize}

\section{The model: when cancellation helps and when it hurts}
For one feature with radiation pattern $\mathbf b$ and real strength $q$, the radiated power is
$P\propto\|\mathbf A_c+q\mathbf b\|^2$ (the norm is the integral over all directions). The best strength and the best
possible reduction are
\begin{equation}
q_*=-\frac{\mathrm{Re}\langle\mathbf b,\mathbf A_c\rangle}{\|\mathbf b\|^2},\qquad
\eta^2=\frac{[\mathrm{Re}\langle\mathbf b,\mathbf A_c\rangle]^2}{\|\mathbf b\|^2\|\mathbf A_c\|^2},\qquad
\frac{P(q)}{P_c}=1-\eta^2+\eta^2\Bigl(\frac{q}{q_*}-1\Bigr)^2 .
\end{equation}
$\eta^2$ is the fraction of the loss this feature can remove: the overlap between the leak and what the feature can
radiate. Three consequences:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item \textbf{Ceiling.} Cancelling a channel that holds a fraction $f$ of the loss gives at most $Q/(1-f)$: 90\,\% gives
$10\times$, 50\,\% gives $2\times$, 8\,\% only $1.09\times$.
\item \textbf{It can hurt.} Above $q=2q_*$ or with the wrong sign the loss is higher than without the feature. This is
likely when the feature radiates into other channels too, when the target channel is small (small safe window), or when
the feature changes the mode width and restoring it changes $\mathbf A_c$. Example from v6.2: a pair at 0.75\,$\mu$m sized
to cancel the cavity's $l=3$ puts 4\,\% of its power into $l=3$ and 63\,\% into $l=2$ and $l=4$. For TE a centre hole is
also a strong detuner, because $E_y$ peaks on the axis.
\item \textbf{Several knobs.} With knobs $\mathbf q$, response matrix $\mathsf B=\partial\mathbf A/\partial\mathbf q$ and the
two constraints (fixed width, fixed resonance) removed by a null space $\mathsf N$, the problem is a small least squares
solve, $\min\|\mathbf A_c+\mathsf{BN}\mathbf z\|^2$. Cancelling $K$ complex channels needs at least $2K+2$ real knobs.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Is there a better basis than spherical harmonics?} (asked of both Claude and GPT, both agree). A change of basis
can always put the whole leak into one term ($\mathbf A_c/\|\mathbf A_c\|$), but it cannot change how well a real knob
overlaps it. The useful ``one term'' is a \emph{controllable} pattern: take the singular value decomposition of
$\mathsf{BN}$; if one singular vector carries most of the leak, its partner vector is literally the list of tooth
changes to make. That is Johnson's idea in the form that fits this device. Spherical harmonics stay useful as a
diagnostic: they tell how compact the source is.

\section{Measuring the far field: the error and the fix}
\fig{1.0}{farfield_v7_method_and_budget.png}{Left: power that one flat monitor projects, divided by the exact value, for
a dipole at the centre of our monitor geometry (D). It is right only within about $40^\circ$ of the monitor normal; the
two monitors were stitched at $45^\circ$, exactly where both are wrong. A bigger box is worse. Right: corrected loss
budget (M). Blue: power radiated by the cavity through the surface around it; grey: the rest of $1-T-R$.}
The far field was computed from two flat monitors, one above and one beside the waveguide, and stitched together at
$45^\circ$. Where both should agree they did not (power ratio 1.3 for TE, 2.3 for TM). The cause (Fig.~1, left): a flat
monitor 2.15\,$\mu$m from the axis and $\pm3.4$\,$\mu$m wide misses the light that leaves at more than about $40^\circ$ from
its normal. \textbf{Fix:} the two monitors cross, so together (with their mirror images) they form a closed tube around
the waveguide. Its $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf H$ give the far field in one coherent calculation, with no seam. The method
reproduces exact dipoles to 0.2\,\%, was reviewed by GPT (one bug found and fixed), and each result carries a check: the
far field power equals the flux through the tube (1.000, 0.986, 0.974 for A, B, C). The three devices were re-run on
IGUM with the same numerics; $T$, wavelength, linewidth and width reproduce exactly.

\textbf{A new finding came with it} (Fig.~1, right): only part of the port loss $1-T-R$ is cavity radiation. The rest,
1.2 to 1.9 points in every device, most likely leaves at the two grating ends, which are outside the tube (E; one run
with longer monitors checks it). This matters for B in particular: it radiates 0.69\,\% of the input from the cavity,
not 2.6\,\%.

\section{Why one-harmonic cancellation fails, and what the leak is instead}
\fig{1.0}{farfield_v7_origin_and_source.png}{Left: the plain TE harmonic content for the same far field, expanded about
three origins that differ by 0.19\,$\mu$m (D). Right: fraction of each device's leak pattern that sources confined
to $\pm L$ around the $\pi$ shift can reproduce, versus $L$ (D, frozen field fit, a ceiling).}
\textbf{The harmonic table depends on a choice} (Fig.~5, left). Moving the expansion centre by 0.19\,$\mu$m, less than
half a pitch, changes the dipole from 3 to 19\,\% and $l=3$ from 41 to 23\,\%. Our cavity has no natural centre on the
0.17\,$\mu$m scale that matters, so ``which harmonic dominates'' is not a physical statement here.

\textbf{The leak is a compact source at the junction} (Fig.~5, right). Fitting the measured complex far field with
dipoles placed along the waveguide: for the plain TE device, sources within $\pm0.5$\,$\mu$m of the $\pi$ shift reproduce
83\,\% of the pattern, and going out to $\pm10$\,$\mu$m adds only 5 points. A random-phase control gives 0. Fitting one
part of the sphere and predicting another without refitting works in two of three tests. For the overshoot device the
curve keeps rising to $\pm10$\,$\mu$m (distributed source); for TM it saturates near 45\,\% (the needle is not a local
source). Two cautions: this is a ceiling for idealised currents, real tooth changes couple several components and are
bounded; and which current component radiates is not yet established.

\textbf{What the earlier experiments mean in this light.}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item \emph{TM comb.} The model predicts the emission angle from the comb period: $u_x=-0.989$ for 531\,nm. Measured: 531
locked ($+16.3\,\%$ $Q$ at fixed width), 536 slightly worse, 545 harmful, 551 no effect. The transmission versus comb
offset is a pure first harmonic ($T(0)+T(180)=T(90)+T(270)=1.7383$), as the phase $e^{i\phi_2}$ requires; lock at $270^\circ$.
It works because it is aimed at TM's needle.
\item \emph{TE post pair} near the centre (historical data): loss 0.119 to 0.093 at fixed width, but the far field change
is a uniform scaling of the whole pattern: the cavity field changed (second term of Eq.~4), not a channel cancelled.
\item \emph{On-axis holes and posts} reach only 2 to 5\,\% of the TE leak (D); v6.2's estimate of ``at most about 10\,\%
for small features'' holds for these, not for the junction teeth.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Mode width must stay fixed.} In a uniform mirror the field decays as $e^{-\gamma|x|}$ with
$\gamma=\sqrt{\kappa^2-\delta_\beta^2}$ (width $\ln2/\gamma$). A feature changes the width in two ways: by shifting the
resonance (second order at the gap centre, about 0.6\,\% per nm), and, if it repeats with the pitch, by changing the
coupling $\kappa$ at first order (oxide holes in the narrow segments shorten the mode, in the wide segments lengthen it,
measured with the predicted signs). Cladding posts at 1.5 to 2\,$\mu$m change neither. A centre feature is compensated
most cheaply by the gap length. Mode width alone sets no lower bound on the leak: at the same 20\,$\mu$m width, a
Gaussian envelope has $10^{-10}$ times less light cone content than the plain exponential one (D); the practical limits
are the coupling a Gaussian needs at its edges, the joins and the finite length.

\section{The Q3dB devices}
At the $-3$\,dB point the loaded $Q$ is a fixed fraction of the radiation $Q$:
$Q_{3\rm dB}=(1-1/\sqrt2)\,Q_i=0.2929\,Q_i$ (reproduces our four measured anchors to about 2\,\%). Length only sets how
strongly the cavity couples to the bus.

\textbf{Harmonic percentages when lengthened: expected unchanged} (E, within 1 to 2 points). The percentages describe the
shape of the radiation, and the shape is set at the junction (Sec.~8). Adding mirror periods at the ends changes the
coupling, not the junction field, so the same pattern is radiated, only more of it: the radiated share of the input goes
from 7.3\,\% at $N=98$ to about 41\,\% at $-3$\,dB. Supporting this: with the end loss removed, the radiation $Q$ of the
plain TE device is $4.35\times10^4$ at $N=98$ and $4.41\times10^4$ at the Q3dB length $N=166$ (D). Needs one run to
confirm (Sec.~11).

\textbf{What a cancellation is worth.} Removing a fraction $f$ of the leak gives $Q_i/(1-f)$, and the device must be
lengthened by $\ln[1/(1-f)]/(2\kappa\Lambda)$ periods per side to return to $-3$\,dB. From the TE Q3dB device
($Q=12\,903$, $N=166$):
\begin{center}\small\begin{tabular}{lcccc}\hline
removed fraction $f$ & 0.1 & 0.3 & 0.5 & 0.8\\\hline
predicted $Q_{3\rm dB}$ (E) & 14\,300 & 18\,400 & 25\,800 & 64\,500\\
extra periods per side & 3 & 10 & 20 & 47\\\hline
\end{tabular}\end{center}
For scale, the TM comb pair device reached $f\approx0.23$ (18\,093 against 13\,930). Above $Q\approx5\times10^4$ the
simulation itself needs a finer wavelength grid and longer run time.

\section{Plan (nothing dispatched)}
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item \textbf{Plain TE, $N=98$}, monitors covering the whole device, plus the near field within $\pm2$\,$\mu$m of the
$\pi$ shift. Settles the end loss and measures the junction source directly.
\item \textbf{Plain TE, $N=166$}, same far field method. Pass if every harmonic group is within 2 points of the $N=98$
table.
\item \textbf{One perturbation} of the innermost teeth, chosen by the least squares model; compare predicted and
simulated radiation, resonance, width. Decides between the 83\,\% ceiling and the 10\,\% estimate.
\item Only if 3 agrees: four to six junction knobs (about ten short runs), solve, validate on one new geometry, then one
Q3dB device.
\item Overshoot device: give the inverse design the radiated power as its objective. TM: longer monitors, then the comb.
\end{enumerate}
Calibration rules from v6.2: track the resonance, check both mesh levels, measure the noise floor, include a control
run; a higher peak $T$ alone is not evidence (it can come from changed coupling), so report $Q_i$, $Q_c$, width and
resonance together.
